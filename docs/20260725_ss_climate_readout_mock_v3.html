Executive summary

This is a genuinely climate-fragility-aware operation whose central risk is that the same Sudd flood pulse it depends on for fish productivity is also South Sudan's single largest driver of displacement. The design is strong where it matters most in this context — it delivers through community institutions rather than a weak centre, and explicitly reaches refugee and host communities — but it has not yet operationalised the compound pathway by which a flood pushes fishing communities into the contested areas of Upper Nile and Jonglei where the project actually works.

Operational context

The FCV setting

South Sudan remains in a fragile transition. The 2018 R-ARCSS peace agreement is in its third extension, elections are deferred to December 2026, and renewed clashes in early 2025 struck Upper Nile and Jonglei — the exact states where the project's fisheries sites sit. Around 1.9 million people are internally displaced and over a million more have arrived from Sudan since 2023, concentrating pressure on the northern host communities the project intends to reach. An oil-dependent economy in near-collapse — 233% inflation, roughly 198% currency depreciation, and a 7.8% GDP contraction after the 2024 pipeline shutdown — has hollowed out the state's delivery capacity, and violence at the sub-national level is very often a fight over natural resources: land, grazing, water and fisheries. That last point matters directly here, because it means the resources this project governs are themselves contested terrain, and very little external assistance flows through government — which is why a community-based delivery model is the only credible route.

The climate setting

South Sudan ranks second globally on the 2025 INFORM Risk Index and is one of the fastest-warming countries on earth. It has seen six major floods and two severe droughts in fifteen years, with over a million people affected by flooding every year since 2019 — and the CCDR is blunt that floods now displace more people than conflict does, and that today's floods are the "new normal." At the centre of it is the Sudd, whose annual flood pulse is simultaneously the country's greatest climate hazard and the foundation of its most productive natural resource, inland fisheries. The CCDR frames climate as a "threat multiplier" on an already fragile system, and its two priority actions for exactly this landscape — flood-risk management and resilient rural livelihoods — are the ones this project is built around.

Where they meet

The project lives at the point where these two systems collide, and the collisions are specific rather than abstract. When the Sudd floods, it does not only lift fish catch — it pushes fishing communities off their land and into areas contested by armed groups, so a climate shock becomes a security-and-displacement crisis in the same moment, at the same six sites where Component 1 is trying to stand up co-management. Drought runs the other way: it drives pastoralist herd movements into the community wildlife conservancy boundaries that Component 2 plans to establish, so a dry year can turn a conservation boundary into a flashpoint and the project's own rangers into perceived parties to a local dispute. And underneath both, the macroeconomic collapse quietly erodes the value-chain economics that the fisheries infrastructure in Sub-component 1.2 depends on — cold storage and better landing sites only pay off if traders can still afford to move and buy. Together these are not risks bolted onto a development project; they are its operating environment.

Strengths & weaknesses — at a glance

Where the design is strong

  • Community delivery (BFMUs, CWCs) fits a weak, contested centre and can adapt to the flood/drought cycle faster than a ministry.
  • Engages displacement directly — refugee and host co-management in Ruweng State.
  • Women's inclusion built into governance — a 40% membership floor, not just a beneficiary count.
  • Names the flood paradox — recognises flooding both boosts fish and displaces communities.

Where it is not yet resolved

  • Flood-displacement compound risk is named but has no design response attached.
  • "Climate-resilient" infrastructure is not set against a realistic 20–50 year flood horizon.
  • No actor-level conflict read of the six fisheries sites.
  • Drought-versus-conservancy friction is unmanaged in Component 2.
Core climate-FCV questions

The questions the climate-FCV frameworks ask of any operation in a fragile, climate-exposed setting. Only the ones material to this project are answered; each is grounded in specific components.

Q1How could climate and FCV affect the project?climate & FCV → project

Flooding is the pathway most likely to interrupt delivery. In the Sudd corridor of Upper Nile and Jonglei, a bad flood does more than damage a landing site — it displaces the very fishing communities the project relies on to run its Boma Fisheries Management Units, and pushes them into areas controlled by armed groups, so physical access and local governance fail together. The design partly anticipates this through flood-resilient construction standards, but three things aren't pinned down: whether those standards are set against realistic multi-decade flood projections, whether emergency financing could be released for a flood-driven displacement, and how traders would reach cold-storage sites when routes are insecure.

A quieter second pathway runs through drought: reduced grazing drives pastoralist movement across the new conservancy boundaries in Component 2, turning a climate stress into an inter-communal — and potentially project-mediated — conflict at exactly the sites the project is standing up.

Q2How could the project affect climate and FCV dynamics?project → climate & FCV

Handled well, the project's governance layer is its biggest contribution back to peace and resilience. Because Boma Fisheries Management Units register fishers and set shared rules across communities, they give people on different sides of a contested resource a common, rules-based forum — a small but real piece of local social cohesion where resource disputes are a live conflict driver. But the sign of this effect is not fixed: built carelessly, the same institutions could entrench one group's access or let ranger structures in Component 2 act as parties to a dispute. What tips it positive is concrete and partly already there — the 40% women's floor, the refugee-host framing in Ruweng, and transparent registration — which is why protecting those features is itself a conflict-sensitivity measure.

Q3Could the design lock in maladaptation?Partial gap

The clearest maladaptation risk sits in the fisheries infrastructure. The landing sites and cold storage financed under Sub-component 1.2 are described as climate-resilient but not yet specified against realistic 20–50 year Sudd flood projections; building to today's flood regime in a fastest-warming country risks assets that are stranded or under water within their own service life. There is a subtler lock-in too: concentrating co-management and infrastructure at today's most productive landing sites can entrench access patterns that a shifting flood pulse later makes inequitable or simply unviable. The do-no-harm-over-time move is to name the flood horizon explicitly now and keep siting decisions adjustable rather than fixed.

Q4Does it engage root causes and create peace & social dividends?Under-claimed

The project can produce positive spillovers — climate, peace and social dividends — even where that wasn't the explicit intent, and small design tweaks could maximise them. It engages a genuine root cause rather than only outputs: contested natural-resource governance is itself a conflict driver in South Sudan, and giving it a rules-based community layer is a structural contribution, not a by-product. Three dividends are within reach. Boma Fisheries Management Units can double as a neutral reconciliation forum at flashpoint landing sites — maximised by an explicit, light dispute-resolution remit and deliberately cross-group membership. The refugee-host shared fishery in Ruweng turns competition into a common interest — maximised by framing some access rights and infrastructure as jointly held. And the 40% women's floor is at once a social and a climate-adaptation dividend, because the people who manage day-to-day resource use are also best placed to read the flood cycle. The project currently claims little of this; naming it would strengthen its responsiveness story.

Q5Are the most vulnerable reached, and through the right institutions?Strong, if protected

The project reaches the people this crisis hits hardest, through the right institutional choice for the context. Its footprint lands on displaced and refugee/host communities in Ruweng and on women, who carry the sharpest edge of a food-insecurity crisis that is already gendered — and it delivers through community bodies (BFMUs, community wildlife conservancies) rather than a weak centre, with a UNHCR partnership anchoring the humanitarian-development nexus in the north. The open question is whether inclusion survives stress: when a flood or a clash scatters a committee, do the women's floor and the refugee-host access rights hold, or quietly erode? Writing those guarantees into the co-management rules is what converts a strong design feature into a durable one.

Q6Is the design adaptive to uncertainty, across realistic time horizons?Gap

This is the least-developed core question, and the most consequential for a second-on-the-INFORM-index country. The design is largely static against a context defined by uncertainty: there is no adaptive trigger for a flood that displaces committees mid-implementation, no explicit handling of the different time horizons in play — a near-term clash, a project-lifetime flood cycle, an asset-lifetime climate shift — and the results framework is not yet set up to track leading indicators of either climate or conflict change. Here, adaptive management is not a refinement to add later; it is the core operating competency the project needs from the start.

Priority action areas

1 · Build the flood-displacement compound risk into the design

HighSensitivity + ResponsivenessRequired before appraisal

Why it matters. A flood that displaces fishing communities is the single most likely climate-FCV shock to this operation, and it hits Component 1's co-management model directly — yet the design currently treats flooding only as an infrastructure-resilience question.

What to do. Specify the Sub-component 1.2 landing sites and cold storage against realistic 20–50 year Sudd flood projections, and add an adaptive trigger and continuity plan for how Boma Fisheries Management Units keep functioning — and how any contingency financing could be released — when a flood scatters their members.

The gap. Flood resilience is named but not operationalised, and the displacement dimension of flooding is absent from the delivery model.

2 · Commission an actor-level conflict analysis for the six fisheries sites

HighSensitivityRequired before appraisal

Why it matters. In active-conflict geographies like Upper Nile and Jonglei, who sits on a Boma Fisheries Management Unit and who controls access is itself a distributional decision that can advantage one faction — the core Do-No-Harm exposure in this project.

What to do. Before establishing BFMUs, map the specific actors and inter-communal dynamics at each of the six sites (drawing on an RRA or equivalent), and use that map to set balanced membership and access rules. Sequence site entry to the security reality rather than a uniform rollout.

The gap. The PCN names categories of risk but cites no site-level actor mapping or conflict diagnostic.

3 · Make adaptive management the design's operating competency

MediumSensitivity + ResponsivenessFlag for preparation

Why it matters. In a second-on-the-INFORM-index setting, a static design cannot hold; the project needs to sense and respond to climate and conflict change, including the drought-versus-conservancy friction in Component 2.

What to do. Build leading climate- and conflict-indicators into the results framework, add drought-adaptive grazing and de-escalation protocols to conservancy design, and give the project explicit stop/go and re-sequencing provisions tied to those indicators.

The gap. The design is largely static, with no adaptive triggers and no treatment of the different climate and conflict time horizons.
