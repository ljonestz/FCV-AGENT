Executive summary

This is a genuinely climate-fragility-aware operation whose central risk is that the same Sudd flood pulse it depends on for fish productivity is also South Sudan's single largest driver of displacement. The design is strong where it matters most in this context — it delivers through community institutions rather than a weak centre, and explicitly reaches refugee and host communities — but it has not yet operationalised the compound pathway by which a flood pushes fishing communities into the contested areas of Upper Nile and Jonglei where the project actually works.

Operational context

The FCV setting

South Sudan remains in a fragile transition. The 2018 R-ARCSS peace agreement is in its third extension, elections are deferred to December 2026, and renewed clashes in early 2025 struck Upper Nile and Jonglei — the exact states where the project's fisheries sites sit. Around 1.9 million people are internally displaced and over a million more have arrived from Sudan since 2023, concentrating pressure on the northern host communities the project intends to reach. An oil-dependent economy in near-collapse — 233% inflation, roughly 198% currency depreciation, and a 7.8% GDP contraction after the 2024 pipeline shutdown — has hollowed out the state's delivery capacity, and violence at the sub-national level is very often a fight over natural resources: land, grazing, water and fisheries. That last point matters directly here, because it means the resources this project governs are themselves contested terrain, and very little external assistance flows through government — which is why a community-based delivery model is the only credible route.

The climate setting

South Sudan ranks second globally on the 2025 INFORM Risk Index and is one of the fastest-warming countries on earth. It has seen six major floods and two severe droughts in fifteen years, with over a million people affected by flooding every year since 2019 — and the CCDR is blunt that floods now displace more people than conflict does, and that today's floods are the "new normal." At the centre of it is the Sudd, whose annual flood pulse is simultaneously the country's greatest climate hazard and the foundation of its most productive natural resource, inland fisheries. The CCDR frames climate as a "threat multiplier" on an already fragile system, and its two priority actions for exactly this landscape — flood-risk management and resilient rural livelihoods — are the ones this project is built around.

Where they meet

The project lives at the point where these two systems collide, and the collisions are specific rather than abstract. When the Sudd floods, it does not only lift fish catch — it pushes fishing communities off their land and into areas contested by armed groups, so a climate shock becomes a security-and-displacement crisis in the same moment, at the same six sites where Component 1 is trying to stand up co-management. Drought runs the other way: it drives pastoralist herd movements into the community wildlife conservancy boundaries that Component 2 plans to establish, so a dry year can turn a conservation boundary into a flashpoint and the project's own rangers into perceived parties to a local dispute. And underneath both, the macroeconomic collapse quietly erodes the value-chain economics that the fisheries infrastructure in Sub-component 1.2 depends on — cold storage and better landing sites only pay off if traders can still afford to move and buy. Together these are not risks bolted onto a development project; they are its operating environment.

Strengths & weaknesses — climate and FCV

Where the design is strong

It delivers through community institutions, which answers both problems at once. Boma Fisheries Management Units and community wildlife conservancies are the right instrument in a setting where the centre cannot deliver and assistance rarely flows through government (the FCV logic), and where local bodies can also read and respond to the flood and drought cycle far faster than a central ministry could (the climate logic). The same design choice addresses the fragility problem and the adaptation problem, which is unusual and genuinely valuable here.
It engages displacement directly, not around the edges. Extending fisheries co-management to refugee and host communities in Ruweng State — including the UNHCR-partnered work under Sub-component 1.4 — meets the humanitarian-development interface where climate and conflict displacement actually land, and does so in the north where arrivals from Sudan are concentrated. Few resource projects in this context design for the displaced as participants rather than bystanders.
It builds women's inclusion into the institutions, not just the beneficiary count. A 40% female-membership floor in the co-management bodies addresses an exclusion that conflict deepened and that climate stress intensifies — as food becomes scarcer, women travel further and girls leave school. Because women already dominate fish processing and NTFP collection, embedding them in governance is both a fairness measure and a functional one: it puts the people who manage day-to-day resource use inside the decisions.
It already names the flood paradox honestly. The PCN recognises that flooding both boosts fish productivity and displaces communities — an unusually candid reading of the core climate-fragility tension for a concept-stage document, and the foundation the rest of the design should build on.

Where it is not yet resolved

The compound flood-displacement pathway is named but not operational. The design sees that floods displace fishers, but there is no adaptive trigger, contingency, or continuity plan for what the Boma Fisheries Management Units do when a flood scatters their members mid-implementation — so the single most likely climate-FCV shock to the operation has no design response attached to it, only an acknowledgement.
"Climate-resilient" infrastructure is not yet pinned to a flood horizon. The landing sites and cold storage under Sub-component 1.2 are described as climate-resilient but not specified against realistic 20–50 year Sudd flood projections. Building to today's flood regime in a fastest-warming country where floods are the "new normal" is a lock-in and maladaptation risk over the assets' service life — a facility optimally sited now could be stranded or submerged well within its lifetime.
There is no actor-level conflict read of the specific sites. The six fisheries locations sit in or beside active-conflict zones in Upper Nile and Jonglei, but the design speaks in risk categories (intercommunal conflict, elite capture) rather than naming the actual armed or political actors present. Without that, it cannot yet check that BFMU membership or access rules won't hand one faction an advantage — the core Do-No-Harm exposure in this project.
The drought-versus-conservancy pathway is unmanaged. Component 2's boundary-setting and ranger structures could themselves become conflict actors during drought-driven pastoralist movement — a documented dynamic in the Boma-Badingilo landscape — and nothing in the current design mediates that friction.
Core climate-FCV questions

The section below explores a set of core questions about how this project connects to climate, peace and social considerations, written in plain terms for a general reader. The questions are drawn from an established body of climate-and-FCV literature, both within the World Bank and beyond it — in particular the Maximizing the Peace and Social Dividends of Climate Action report, the FCV-Sensitive Climate Action Framework, and the Defueling Conflict (peace and social dividends) series. From that broader battery of questions, the readout surfaces only the ones that speak most directly to this project, and answers each against the project's own components and context.

Q1How could climate and FCV affect the project?climate & FCV → project

Flooding is the pathway most likely to interrupt delivery. In the Sudd corridor of Upper Nile and Jonglei, a bad flood does more than damage a landing site — it displaces the very fishing communities the project relies on to run its Boma Fisheries Management Units, and pushes them into areas controlled by armed groups, so physical access and local governance fail together in the same season. The design partly anticipates this through the flood-resilient construction standards in Sub-component 1.2, but three things aren't pinned down, and each is a specific, answerable design question rather than a general warning: whether those standards are set against realistic multi-decade flood projections rather than the historical record; whether any contingency financing could actually be released quickly for a flood-driven displacement; and how traders would reach cold-storage sites when the routes between them are insecure.

A second, quieter pathway runs through drought and through the wider economy. Reduced grazing drives pastoralist movement across the new conservancy boundaries in Component 2, turning a climate stress into an inter-communal — and potentially project-mediated — conflict at exactly the sites the project is standing up. And beneath both hazards, the macroeconomic collapse erodes the value-chain economics that Sub-component 1.2's infrastructure depends on: a climate-resilient landing site and cold store deliver little if 233% inflation has priced traders out of moving fish — and with roughly 70% of the fishery's value already lost to post-harvest inefficiency, the margin the project is trying to capture is thin before a single shock lands. The project's real exposure, then, is compound — a single bad flood or dry season can hit livelihoods, security, and market viability at once.

Grounded in: FCV-Sensitive Climate Action Framework; Defueling Conflict.

Q2How could the project affect climate and FCV dynamics?project → climate & FCV

Handled well, the project's governance layer is its biggest contribution back to peace and resilience. Because Boma Fisheries Management Units register fishers, enforce local rules, and liaise with state authorities under Sub-component 1.1, they give people on different sides of a contested resource a common, rules-based forum — a small but real piece of local social cohesion in a place where disputes over fisheries, grazing and water are a live conflict driver. Where the design leans in — the 40% women's membership floor, the refugee-host framing in Ruweng, transparent registration — it points this effect firmly toward inclusion and away from capture.

But the sign of this contribution is not fixed, and that is the part the design has to actively manage. Built carelessly, the same institutions could entrench one group's access — a dominant clan controlling the best landing sites through BFMU membership — or let the ranger and enforcement structures in Component 2 act as parties to a dispute, a real risk in the Boma-Badingilo landscape where Dinka, Murle and Nuer territorial dynamics are documented conflict drivers. What tips it positive is concrete and largely already in the design; protecting those features under pressure is therefore itself a conflict-sensitivity measure, not a soft add-on, and it belongs in the project's operational rules rather than its aspirations.

Grounded in: Maximizing the Peace and Social Dividends of Climate Action; Conflict-Sensitive Climate Action Compendium.

Q3Could the design lock in maladaptation?Partial gap

The clearest maladaptation risk sits in the fisheries infrastructure. The landing sites and cold storage financed under Sub-component 1.2 are described as climate-resilient but not yet specified against realistic 20–50 year Sudd flood projections. In a fastest-warming country where the CCDR expects today's extreme floods to become the norm, building to the historical flood regime risks assets that are stranded or under water within their own service life — a cold store placed at a currently-optimal landing point could be cut off or destroyed if the flood pulse moves, which is precisely the kind of hard-infrastructure lock-in the frameworks warn against.

There is a subtler, institutional lock-in too. Concentrating co-management and infrastructure at today's most productive sites — and formalising fisher rights through BFMU registration around current fishing grounds — can entrench access patterns that a shifting flood pulse later makes inequitable or simply unviable, because the fish and the people will move even if the registers do not. The do-no-harm-over-time move is to name the flood horizon explicitly in the design now, keep siting decisions adjustable rather than fixed in concrete, and structure BFMU rights so they can travel with communities rather than being tied to a geography the climate is actively rearranging.

Grounded in: FCV-Sensitive Climate Action Framework (maladaptation and lock-in).

Q4Does it engage root causes and create peace & social dividends?Under-claimed

The project can produce positive spillovers — climate, peace and social dividends — even where that was not the explicit intent, and small design tweaks could maximise them. These are opportunities, not requirements: places where the project could deliver more peace and resilience value than it currently claims. It starts from a strong position because it engages a genuine root cause rather than only outputs — contested natural-resource governance is itself a conflict driver in South Sudan, and giving it a rules-based community layer through BFMUs and community wildlife conservancies is a structural contribution to stability, not a by-product of a fisheries project.

Three dividends are within concrete reach. First, the Boma Fisheries Management Units can double as a neutral reconciliation forum at flashpoint landing sites — maximised by giving them an explicit, light-touch dispute-resolution remit and making sure membership deliberately spans the groups that actually compete over each site, rather than forming around whoever is easiest to convene. Second, the refugee-host shared fishery in Ruweng (Sub-component 1.4) turns two groups' competition into a common economic interest — maximised by framing some access rights and infrastructure as jointly held rather than allocated to one community and used by the other. Third, the 40% women's floor is at once a social and a climate-adaptation dividend, because the people who manage day-to-day resource use are also best placed to read and respond to the flood cycle. The project currently claims almost none of this explicitly; naming these pathways would strengthen its responsiveness story and give real content to the PDO's "enhanced resilience" indicator.

Grounded in: Maximizing the Peace and Social Dividends of Climate Action.

Q5Are the most vulnerable reached, and through the right institutions?Strong, if protected

The project reaches the people this crisis hits hardest, through the right institutional choice for the context. Its footprint lands on displaced and refugee/host communities in Ruweng and on women, who carry the sharpest edge of a food-insecurity crisis that is already gendered — and the PDO's disaggregated indicators (share female, refugee, host) show that inclusion is being tracked, not just asserted. Crucially, it delivers through community bodies — Boma Fisheries Management Units, community wildlife conservancies — rather than a weak centre, with the UNHCR partnership under Sub-component 1.4 anchoring the humanitarian-development nexus in the north, which is the correct read of a context where little assistance flows through government.

The open question is whether inclusion and the institutions themselves survive stress. When a flood or a clash scatters a committee, do the women's floor and the refugee-host access rights hold, or quietly erode back to the pre-project pattern? And the community-based model's great strength — independence from a fragile centre — is also a dependency: BFMUs and conservancies still need enough linkage to state structures (the National–State Fisheries Working Group re-established under Sub-component 1.1) to endure and to hand off to, without over-relying on institutions that may not be there. Writing the inclusion guarantees into the co-management rules, and deliberately calibrating the community-to-state balance, is what turns a strong design feature into a durable one.

Grounded in: FCV-Sensitive Climate Action Framework; Conflict-Sensitive Climate Action Compendium (inclusion and institutions).

Q6Is the design adaptive to uncertainty, across realistic time horizons?Gap

This is the least-developed core question, and the most consequential for a second-on-the-INFORM-index country. The design is largely static against a context defined by uncertainty. There is no adaptive trigger for a flood that displaces committees mid-implementation, no explicit handling of the different time horizons in play — a near-term clash that closes a site for a season, a project-lifetime flood cycle that shifts where the fish are, an asset-lifetime climate shift that outlives the infrastructure — and the results architecture under Component 3 is not yet set up to track leading indicators of either climate or conflict change.

Concretely, the PDO-level indicators measure outputs — hectares under enhanced management, beneficiaries reached, CBNRM organisations established — but not the context change that would tell the team when to adapt, slow down, or re-sequence. In a setting this volatile, adaptive management is not a refinement to add during implementation; it is the core operating competency the project needs from the start: leading climate- and conflict-indicators in the results framework, explicit stop/go and re-sequencing provisions tied to them, drought-adaptive protocols for the Component 2 conservancies, and continuity plans for displaced BFMUs. Without that, a static design will be overtaken by the first bad flood or dry season it meets.

Grounded in: FCV-Sensitive Climate Action Framework; CCDR guidance (adaptive design and horizons).

Priority action areas

1 · Build the flood-displacement compound risk into the design

HighSensitivity + ResponsivenessRequired before appraisal

Why it matters. A flood that displaces fishing communities is the single most likely climate-FCV shock to this operation, and it hits Component 1's co-management model directly — yet the design currently treats flooding only as an infrastructure-resilience question.

What to do. Specify the Sub-component 1.2 landing sites and cold storage against realistic 20–50 year Sudd flood projections, and add an adaptive trigger and continuity plan for how Boma Fisheries Management Units keep functioning — and how any contingency financing could be released — when a flood scatters their members.

The gap. Flood resilience is named but not operationalised, and the displacement dimension of flooding is absent from the delivery model.

2 · Commission an actor-level conflict analysis for the six fisheries sites

HighSensitivityRequired before appraisal

Why it matters. In active-conflict geographies like Upper Nile and Jonglei, who sits on a Boma Fisheries Management Unit and who controls access is itself a distributional decision that can advantage one faction — the core Do-No-Harm exposure in this project.

What to do. Before establishing BFMUs, map the specific actors and inter-communal dynamics at each of the six sites (drawing on an RRA or equivalent), and use that map to set balanced membership and access rules. Sequence site entry to the security reality rather than a uniform rollout.

The gap. The PCN names categories of risk but cites no site-level actor mapping or conflict diagnostic.

3 · Make adaptive management the design's operating competency

MediumSensitivity + ResponsivenessFlag for preparation

Why it matters. In a second-on-the-INFORM-index setting, a static design cannot hold; the project needs to sense and respond to climate and conflict change, including the drought-versus-conservancy friction in Component 2.

What to do. Build leading climate- and conflict-indicators into the Component 3 results framework, add drought-adaptive grazing and de-escalation protocols to conservancy design, and give the project explicit stop/go and re-sequencing provisions tied to those indicators.

The gap. The design is largely static, with no adaptive triggers and no treatment of the different climate and conflict time horizons.
